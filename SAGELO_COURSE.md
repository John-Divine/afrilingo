## About This Book

This is a complete, self-contained course in **Sagelo**, a constructed language designed from the ground up to be pleasant to hear, gentle to speak, fast to learn, and — unlike most languages you'll ever study — quietly built to sharpen the way you think.

You do not need a teacher, a classroom, or any prior knowledge of linguistics to use this book. Every lesson stands on the last. Work through it in order the first time; after that, use it as a lifelong reference.

This book has five parts:

- **Part I — Foundations.** The philosophy, the sounds, and the writing system. Read this first, even if you're eager to jump to grammar — everything after depends on it.
- **Part II — The Thirty Lessons.** A complete, graded course from your first sentence to fluent, complex reasoning in Sagelo. Each lesson introduces one idea, shows it in real sentences, and gives you practice with an answer key.
- **Part III — The Dictionary.** Several hundred Sagelo roots, organized by theme, plus a full English-to-Sagelo index.
- **Part IV — Readings and Culture.** Proverbs, a folktale, songs and mantras, and short essays imagining how a community of Sagelo speakers might live, think, and mark time — because a language is never just grammar; it's a way of being in the world.
- **Part V — Reference Appendices.** Every grammar rule, particle, and irregular word in one place, for quick lookup once you've moved past needing full explanations.

### How to Study

1. Read a lesson slowly, aloud, at least once — Sagelo is a language for the mouth and ear as much as the eye.
2. Do the practice exercises before checking answers. Struggling productively is where learning happens.
3. Revisit Part I's pronunciation and tone sections often. They matter more than they'll feel like they do at first.
4. Don't rush Part II. Thirty lessons sounds like a lot, but each is short by design — better to do one lesson a day, thoroughly, than five in a rush.
5. Once you finish Part II, use Parts III–V as a living reference, the way you'd use a dictionary and grammar guide for any language you're actively using.

# PART I: FOUNDATIONS

## Chapter 1: Why Sagelo Exists, and Where It Comes From

Every natural language on Earth grew the way a river grows — carved slowly by millions of speakers over thousands of years, shaped as much by historical accident, conquest, and migration as by any inner logic. That's part of what makes natural languages beautiful. It's also why they're often *hard*: irregular verbs nobody would design on purpose, spelling systems that stopped matching pronunciation centuries ago, grammatical genders with no discoverable rule behind them.

Sagelo asks a different question: **what if a language were designed, on purpose, the way a well-made tool is designed** — every part chosen because it does a job, nothing left in because "that's just how it ended up"?

To answer that question, Sagelo doesn't invent from nothing. It studies what actually works, across real human languages, and borrows deliberately:

- From **Lonkundo** (Democratic Republic of the Congo) and the wider **Bantu family**, Sagelo takes open, vowel-final syllables and the idea of sorting nouns into meaningful classes — the source of its gentle sound and much of its underlying structure.
- From **Japanese**, **Hawaiian**, and **Swahili**, Sagelo takes a small, universal set of consonants and vowels, so that almost anyone, from almost any linguistic background on Earth, can pronounce it correctly within days.
- From **Esperanto**, Sagelo takes its most powerful labor-saving idea: that a word's grammatical job can be signaled by a single final vowel, letting one root generate five real words instead of forcing you to memorize five unrelated ones.
- From **Toki Pona**, Sagelo takes the instinct toward a small core vocabulary, built to combine rather than sprawl.
- From **Lojban**, the "logical language," Sagelo takes explicit grammatical machinery for logic itself: real, spoken words for AND, OR, NOT, IF/THEN, FOR ALL, and THERE EXISTS.
- From **Quechua**, **Turkish**, and other languages that grammatically mark *evidentiality*, Sagelo takes the habit of never letting a claim pass without silently marking how you know it.
- From **Mandarin Chinese**, Sagelo takes a fully transparent numeral system — no irregular "eleven, twelve, thirteen" to memorize by rote.
- From **Latin and Greek**, the roots buried inside English and Romance-language science vocabulary, Sagelo takes numeral and technical roots that double as a bridge into other languages you might study later.
- From **Sanskrit, Chinese, Polynesian, and Japanese philosophical vocabulary** — *dharma, karma, tao, chi, mana, zen, satya* — Sagelo borrows a handful of words whole and unmodified, out of respect, because these ideas deserved their own names long before Sagelo existed, and translating them into new roots would have erased something real.

The result is not meant to replace any of these languages, or to flatten their differences into sameness. It's meant to be a **doorway**: a language simple and logical enough to learn quickly, but rich enough that learning it warms up the exact mental muscles — pattern recognition, logical structure, numerical transparency, categorical thinking — that make *every other language, and mathematics itself*, easier to learn afterward.

### The name

*Sagelo* — SAH-geh-lo — means "tongue of the sage." It's built from *sag* (wisdom) and *-lo* (tongue/language), following the very word-building rule you'll learn in Lesson 1. Even the name of the language teaches you its own grammar. That's not an accident either.

## Chapter 2: The Sounds of Sagelo

### The five vowels

Sagelo has exactly five vowel sounds — the same five found, in some form, in more of the world's languages than any other vowel set (Spanish, Japanese, Swahili, and hundreds more all use something close to this system).

| Vowel | Sounds like | Practice words |
|---|---|---|
| **a** | "ah" in *father* | *sa, ka, ma, ta, la* |
| **e** | "eh" in *bed* | *se, ke, me, te, le* |
| **i** | "ee" in *feel* | *si, ki, mi, ti, li* |
| **o** | "oh" in *note* | *so, ko, mo, to, lo* |
| **u** | "oo" in *room* | *su, ku, mu, tu, lu* |

### The twenty consonants

| Sound | Sagelo letter(s) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| p, b | p, b | as in English |
| t, d | t, d | as in English |
| k, g | k, g | as in English (always hard g) |
| m, n | m, n | as in English |
| ny | ny | as in Spanish "ñ" / English "canyon" |
| f, v | f, v | as in English |
| s, sh | s, sh | as in English "see," "she" |
| ch, j | ch, j | as in English "church," "judge" |
| h | h | as in English "hat" |
| l, r | l, r | "r" is a light, flapped sound, closer to Spanish or Japanese "r" than English "r" |
| w, y | w, y | as in English |

Notice what's **missing**: no clicks, no ejectives, no uvular sounds, no glottal stops, no heavy consonant clusters like "str-" or "-nths." Every single sound in this list already exists in at least a dozen major world languages.

### Syllable shape: always open

**Every syllable in Sagelo ends in a vowel.** The full pattern is:

> (Consonant) + (w or y glide, optional) + Vowel

So *kwa, kya, gwa, mya* are all valid single syllables (consonant + glide + vowel), but a cluster like *str-* or a syllable ending in a consonant (like English "cat") never occurs.

Kinship loans like *nyango*, *ndeko*, and whole loanwords like *shanti* retain their original smooth pronunciation.

## Chapter 3: Tone — Sagelo's Music

### Two tones

- **High tone**, marked with an acute accent: á, é, í, ó, ú
- **Low tone**, left unmarked

Tone sits on a word's main (root) syllable.
- Low-to-high interval: 3:2 ratio (perfect fifth).
- Downdrift: golden-ratio decay (1/φ ≈ 0.618).
- Stress duration: ~1.6x on stressed syllable.

### Stress: always predictable, and separate from tone

Stress (which syllable you say slightly louder and longer) **always falls on the second-to-last syllable of a word** — a rule with no exceptions, borrowed from Swahili, Polish, and countless Latin-derived words.
